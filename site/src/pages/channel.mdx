# Channel and kit

<p class="lede">A channel is one field of a pose, and it owns the arithmetic: how two voices writing it combine, and what it reads when nobody is writing.</p>

<Channel client:only="react" />
<Source of="Channel.tsx" />

Two voices write the same field. Whether the result is their sum, their product, the larger of the two or simply the later one isn't up to either voice. It belongs to the channel, so a flicker and a fade can share `gain` without knowing about each other.

Every stock channel but `last` has a **rest**: the value that changes nothing. It's 0 for `sum` and `max`, and 1 for `mul`. A voice's weight fades it toward rest, so at weight 0 a voice leaves its channel exactly as it found it. Drag a weight down and watch its bar shrink into the dashed line, and its lamp settle where the channel rests.

`last` has no rest, so there is nothing to fade toward. A weight can only switch it on or off: by default it is on while the weight is above 0. A mix's [`band`](MixOptions.band) sets two thresholds instead, on above one and off below the other, so a weight hovering between them doesn't flicker.

A **kit** names the channels one kind of pose is made of:

```ts
import { color, ink, kit, max, mul, sum, vec } from '@msb235/blits';

const PART = kit<PartPose>({
  gain: mul(),
  dark: max(),
  position: vec(3, sum()),
  color: color(),
  status: ink(),
});
```

`vec(n, of)` runs one channel's arithmetic down an axis list. A consumer can declare a channel of its own: it's four functions, and only `merge` and `lerp` are required.

## More ways to fold

`min()` is `max` turned over: the smallest contribution wins, so the most restrictive voice decides a clip radius or a speed limit. It rests at the top of its bounds, `min({ bounds: [0, 400] })` at 400, or at 1 without any, and a weight fades a contribution toward that rest.

`norm()` sits between `sum` and `max`. Two voices at 1 give 2 under `sum`, 1 under `max`, and about 1.41 under `norm()`: overlapping glows build up a little without piling up. One voice alone gives its own value. `norm({ power })` sets where it sits, `sum` at 1 and closer to `max` the higher it goes; the default is 2.

`affine()` holds a 2D transform as the six numbers of CSS's `matrix()` and canvas's `setTransform`, and `matrix({ x, y, rotate, skew, scale })` builds one, angles in radians. Voices compose in the order they were cued, the first outermost, as a CSS transform list reads: a voice that rotates, cued before one that moves, swings the moved subject round the origin, and cued after it turns the subject where it stands. A weight takes a transform part of the way from none, and a crossfade goes between two by their parts, the rotation the short way round, as CSS interpolates.

```ts
const SPRITE = kit<SpritePose>({ at: affine() });
const spin = patch<Sprite, SpritePose>(2000, (t) => ({ at: matrix({ rotate: t * 2 * Math.PI }) }), {
  writes: ['at'],
});
// each frame: ctx.setTransform(...pose.at)
```

## Two kinds of color

blits has two color channels that take the same values and blend them differently. `oklab(0xrrggbb)` makes a value for either, and `toHex(pose, under)` or `css(pose)` turns a pose of either back into something to draw.

`color()` is color as the real world shows it: light, sky, smoke, paint. Voices stacked on it average straight across OKLab, and it moves between two colors in a straight line there too, so red heading for blue passes a grayed purple, as two lights mixing would.

`ink()` is color standing for a meaning: a status, a level, a team. People picture the midpoint between red and blue as purple, reached by turning the hue, and `ink` gives them that: it moves round the hue, and stacked voices blend round the hue at their average vividness, so red and blue at equal weight make a vivid purple. Two inks with nearly opposite hues swing round the wheel as their weights cross.

The same voices write both rows. On the left one voice sweeps from red to blue and back; on the right a red voice holds while a blue one fades in and out over it. `color(last())` and `ink(last())` replace instead of stacking, and travel the same way their averaging forms do. A tween, spring or glide on an `ink` moves round the hue as well.

## Blend modes

`color(multiply())`, `color(screen())`, `color(add())`, `color(lighten())` and `color(darken())` lay each voice over what is under it the way an image editor's layer modes do, in linear light: a voice is a layer, and its weight times its coverage is the layer's opacity. Multiply darkens, as a filter or a shadow does; screen and add lighten, as projected light does; lighten and darken keep whichever is lighter or darker on each of red, green and blue. They work on `ink` too, which only changes how a value travels between keys.

blits never sees what is under a subject, so the pose holds the one layer the voices add up to, and the host supplies the base: `toHex(pose, under)` blends onto `under`, and `css(pose)` gives the layer with `blendMode(pose)` naming the CSS `mix-blend-mode` to put it on with. The browser blends in gamma-encoded sRGB rather than linear light, so its midtones come out darker than `toHex`'s.

`color(overlay())`, `color(softLight())`, `color(hardLight())` and `color(difference())` work the same way, except that what each layer does depends on what is under it, so the order matters. Overlay and soft light deepen contrast, keeping the base's lights and darks; hard light is overlay with layer and base swapped; difference inverts under white and leaves black alone. The pose keeps every voice's layer, the first one cued at the bottom, and `toHex(pose, under)` lays them on in that order. It has room for four layers a subject, and `overlay({ layers: 8 })` gives more; a voice past that is left off, with a warning. `css(pose)` gives one layer exactly, at its opacity; with several it gives their average, since one CSS color can't stack.

The same stacking holds 16 more. From CSS: `colorDodge()`, `colorBurn()`, `exclusion()`, `hue()`, `saturation()`, `colorMode()` (CSS's `color`, renamed because `color` is the channel it goes in), and `luminosity()`. From image editors: `subtract()`, `divide()`, `linearBurn()`, `vividLight()`, `linearLight()`, `pinLight()`, `hardMix()`, `darkerColor()`, and `lighterColor()`; CSS has none of these, so `blendMode` gives `normal` and only `toHex` lays them. `hue`, `saturation`, `colorMode`, and `luminosity` trade hue, chroma, and lightness in OKLab rather than CSS's HSL-like space, so they don't match the browser's, and over a gray, hue and saturation have no hue to keep. The [blend modes example](../examples/blend-modes/) runs all of them side by side.
